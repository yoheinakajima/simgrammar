# Preface

This book argues that simulation games, for all their variety, are assembled from a shared set of parts: production chains, needs, markets, weather, raids, a pause button. What makes a game itself is a handful of rarer parts and how they are wired together. Naming the parts shows why games feel alike, how old most of their ideas are, what each part costs a designer in computation, writing, and clarity, where AI techniques can change them, and which combinations the catalog has not seen yet. It is written for designers deciding what to build, for players who want to see how the games they love work, and for anyone deciding where AI belongs in a game.

Part I takes the games apart. Part II follows the AI techniques that have reached games and shows where the rest would land. Part III makes dated bets on what will be built, says what could go wrong, and explains how each bet will be scored. Part IV, for readers who want it, describes how the book is made and checked.

The book draws on a catalog of {{count:game}} games, chosen to cover every subgenre of simulation game, every title that started a lineage from the 1960s on, recent releases, research demonstrations that run as interactive simulations, and a few works included for a single mechanic. A game belongs when it was released, commercially, freely, or as a public demo, and its core loop runs on a model of a system, such as an economy, an ecology, a body, or a city, whose state changes without the player scripting each change. It also has to add something: a way of using a mechanic the catalog does not already show well, or a place at the head of a lineage. The catalog favors games that are well documented in English, so mobile management games and some Japanese and Chinese simulation traditions are thinner than they should be. When the book says "in the catalog", it means these games.

Every number in the book comes from that catalog, and the {{stat:n_book_sentences_asserted}} sentences that rank, compare, or group those numbers carry checks that fail the build when the catalog stops supporting them, so the text keeps pace as games are added. The checks test agreement with the catalog; whether my reading of a number is right is still an argument, and the chapters make it in the open. I built the catalog with AI agents doing most of the reading and drafting under written rules, and people and agents now extend it under the same rules. Every game, mechanic, and technique is a public record.
