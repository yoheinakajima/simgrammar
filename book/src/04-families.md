# Chapter 4. Families

In [[factorio|Factorio]] the lights are going out. You added a row of assemblers an hour ago, the steam engines cannot keep up, and every machine on the map has slowed. The belts back up. Smoke from the furnaces has drifted past the forest that used to absorb it, and a nest of biters at the edge of the map is soaking it up. The pollution it absorbs pays for attackers, 4 units for a small biter up to 400 for a behemoth, and a wave is forming.

In [[anno-1800|Anno 1800]] nothing is on fire. A street of artisans' houses sits one step short of advancing to the next tier. They are full, their basic needs are met, and they have the construction materials, but the canned goulash they want comes from a chain that spans two islands and a ship that is late. When the houses do advance, the artisans who move up stop working in the artisan-tier factories, and a different chain starves.

Both games are production economies. Both are about recipes that turn raw materials into goods through chains of buildings, and both reward the player who gets the ratios right. The dataset puts them in one family, with factory games such as [[satisfactory|Satisfactory]], [[shapez-2|shapez 2]], [[dyson-sphere-program|Dyson Sphere Program]], and [[captain-of-industry|Captain of Industry]] and settlement builders such as [[manor-lords|Manor Lords]], [[banished|Banished]], [[caesar-3|Caesar III]], and [[pharaoh|Pharaoh]].{{assert:derived.anno-1800.cluster == derived.factorio.cluster}}{{assert:derived.satisfactory.cluster == derived.factorio.cluster}}{{assert:derived.shapez-2.cluster == derived.factorio.cluster}}{{assert:derived.dyson-sphere-program.cluster == derived.factorio.cluster}}{{assert:derived.captain-of-industry.cluster == derived.factorio.cluster}}{{assert:derived.manor-lords.cluster == derived.anno-1800.cluster}}{{assert:derived.banished.cluster == derived.anno-1800.cluster}}{{assert:derived.caesar-3.cluster == derived.anno-1800.cluster}}{{assert:derived.pharaoh.cluster == derived.anno-1800.cluster}}

## What the two games share

Factorio and Anno 1800 share six mechanics: production chains, storage, building placement, blueprints, scheduled vehicle routes, and a large late-game construction project that soaks up surplus. The two most common are production chains, in {{derived:production-chain.prevalence}} catalog games, and building placement, in {{derived:free-placement.prevalence}}.{{assert:derived.production-chain.prevalence >= 30}}{{assert:derived.free-placement.prevalence >= 60}}

The parts each game has alone are rarer, and they decide what the game feels like. Factorio's include conveyor belts, a power grid, pollution that spreads chunk by chunk, enemies whose evolution rises with the pollution produced, circuit networks, and robots that move items between chests. Anno 1800's include population tiers whose needs gate their advancement, goods consumed per second by every house, trade routes between islands and a second map, and rival companies. Factorio is a game about throughput, where the challenge is moving items fast enough; Anno is a game about demand, where the challenge is what a growing population will want next.

The grouping method is built to weigh rare parts more heavily than common ones, so it sees that difference too: inside the family, Factorio sits much closer to Satisfactory than to Anno 1800.{{assert:sim_factorio_satisfactory > sim_factorio_anno_1800}}

## How the families are drawn

Each game is represented by the set of mechanics it uses. Core mechanics count twice as much as supporting ones. Each mechanic is also weighted by its rarity, the logarithm of the catalog size divided by the number of games that use it, so two games that share a rare part count as much closer than two games that share a pause button. The similarity of two games is the weighted overlap of their sets. The build then groups games by repeatedly joining the most similar groups, measuring similarity between groups as the average over their members, and cuts the resulting tree into a chosen number of clusters.

The weights are steep. A shared storyteller, used by {{derived:storyteller-director.prevalence}} catalog games, carries about {{stat:idf_ratio_storyteller_pause}} times the weight of a shared pause button, before the doubling for core parts.{{assert:idf_ratio_storyteller_pause > 4}} On this measure, where 1 would mean identical parts in identical roles, Factorio and Satisfactory score {{stat:sim_factorio_satisfactory}} and Factorio and Anno 1800 score {{stat:sim_factorio_anno_1800}}. RimWorld scores {{stat:sim_rimworld_dwarf_fortress}} with Dwarf Fortress and {{stat:sim_rimworld_the_sims}} with The Sims, and SimCity scores {{stat:sim_simcity_1989_cities_skylines}} with Cities: Skylines.{{assert:sim_factorio_satisfactory > sim_factorio_anno_1800}}{{assert:sim_rimworld_dwarf_fortress > sim_rimworld_the_sims}} Even Factorio and Satisfactory, in one family, share less than half of their weighted parts, because each carries rare parts the other lacks, and those count for the most.{{assert:sim_factorio_satisfactory < 0.5}}{{assert:derived.satisfactory.cluster == derived.factorio.cluster}}{{assert:sim_simcity_1989_cities_skylines < 0.5}}{{assert:derived.cities-skylines.cluster == derived.simcity-1989.cluster}}

The number of families is set by a fixed rule, so that no one, the author included, can pick the grouping that tells the neatest story. The book then claims only groupings that hold up when the catalog is resampled: two games belong together when they land in the same family almost every time, and stand apart when they almost never do.

<details class="disclosure"><summary>how the number of families is chosen, and when two games belong together</summary>

The number of clusters has to come from somewhere. A designer looking at the result could pick the number that gives the neatest story, and the method is set up so that nobody can. The build takes {{stat:cluster_subsamples}} subsamples, each holding about {{stat:cluster_subsample_pct}} percent of the games, re-clusters each one, and measures how well each re-clustering agrees with the full one, using the adjusted Rand index, a score that is 1 for perfect agreement and near 0 for chance. The stated rule takes the smallest number of clusters whose average agreement reaches {{stat:cluster_min_mean_ari}}. When the rule was last applied it picked {{stat:cluster_k}}, and that number is fixed in the analysis settings so that adding a game does not reshuffle every cluster.

When the data moves far enough that the rule would pick another number, the validator warns and the number is chosen again by the rule; it was last chosen after a correction to the catalog's tags, and it matches the rule today.{{assert:cluster_rule_k == cluster_k}}{{assert:cluster_k_drift == 0}} Small changes to the data can move the rule's pick by several clusters, because the agreement curve is nearly flat over that range: every number of clusters from the high teens to thirty scores close to the threshold. The number of families is therefore a weak fact. Neighbouring choices are about as defensible as the configured one, so the claims worth making are about which groupings survive resampling.

The book uses two thresholds for that. When it says two games belong together, they share a cluster in at least {{stat:cocluster_together_pct}} percent of the subsamples. When it says two games stand apart, they share one in at most {{stat:cocluster_apart_pct}} percent. Anything in between is called a weaker link and pinned by comparison. The build fails if a new game breaks one of these claims. By a separate measure, {{stat:n_stable_clusters}} of the {{stat:n_clusters}} clusters keep an average overlap of at least {{stat:cluster_stable_jaccard}} with their best match across subsamples.{{assert:n_stable_clusters >= 10}}

## Families that cross genres

Most of the families follow genre lines in part and cross them in part. Each crossing pairs games filed under different genres that a designer can study side by side.

**Schedules and verdicts.** [[papers-please|Papers, Please]], a 2013 game about checking passports at a border post, belongs with [[princess-maker-2|Princess Maker 2]], a 1993 game about raising a daughter from age 10 to 18.{{assert:derived.princess-maker-2.cluster == derived.papers-please.cluster}} The resemblance is in the structure. Papers, Please gives you a timed shift, pay per correct decision, and a household ledger of rent, food, and heat that turns speed and accuracy into a moral trade. Princess Maker 2 splits each month into three slots, each spent on training, a job, adventure, or rest, under a small annual income, and her attributes at 18 select one of 74 endings. Both are budgets of scarce time spent against authored events, and the dilemma games [[suzerain|Suzerain]] and [[reigns|Reigns]] sit in the same group.{{assert:derived.suzerain.cluster == derived.reigns.cluster}}{{assert:cocluster.papers-please.suzerain > 0.5}} Princess Maker 2 is labelled a life sim, and it rarely shares a family with [[the-sims|The Sims]].{{assert:cocluster.princess-maker-2.the-sims < 0.15}}

**Machines under your hands.** [[farming-simulator-25|Farming Simulator 25]] belongs with the driving games, [[iracing|iRacing]], [[gran-turismo-7|Gran Turismo 7]], and [[euro-truck-simulator-2|Euro Truck Simulator 2]], and stands apart from the farming games [[stardew-valley|Stardew Valley]] and [[harvest-moon|Harvest Moon]].{{assert:derived.farming-simulator-25.cluster == derived.iracing.cluster}}{{assert:derived.euro-truck-simulator-2.cluster == derived.iracing.cluster}}{{assert:derived.farming-simulator-25.cluster != derived.stardew-valley.cluster}}{{assert:derived.farming-simulator-25.cluster != derived.harvest-moon.cluster}} Its record says why: it models agriculture as ordered field operations with specific implements, across more than 400 licensed vehicles, so the challenge is operating machines and managing a fleet. **Creatures you raise.** [[little-computer-people|Little Computer People]], [[tamagotchi|Tamagotchi]], [[creatures|Creatures]], and [[black-and-white|Black & White]] hold together on care and learning.{{assert:derived.tamagotchi.cluster == derived.creatures.cluster}}{{assert:derived.little-computer-people.cluster == derived.creatures.cluster}}{{assert:derived.black-and-white.cluster == derived.creatures.cluster}} Black & White is a god game, and it stands apart from [[populous|Populous]], the game that founded its genre; the creature you train in it puts it in the same family as the virtual pets.{{assert:derived.black-and-white.cluster != derived.populous.cluster}}

**Crowds you manage.** [[theme-hospital|Theme Hospital]], [[rollercoaster-tycoon|RollerCoaster Tycoon]], and [[game-dev-tycoon|Game Dev Tycoon]] hold together on hiring staff, research, and crowds with preferences.{{assert:derived.rollercoaster-tycoon.cluster == derived.theme-hospital.cluster}}{{assert:derived.game-dev-tycoon.cluster == derived.theme-hospital.cluster}} Their visitors and patients have needs as The Sims' characters do, and they usually land apart from the life sims, because what the player manages is a business serving a crowd.{{assert:cocluster.theme-hospital.the-sims < 0.15}}

**Worlds that keep running.** [[eve-online|EVE Online]] holds together with [[ultima-online|Ultima Online]], [[second-life|Second Life]], and A Tale in the Desert on persistent worlds, player markets, player organizations, and law that players make.{{assert:derived.ultima-online.cluster == derived.eve-online.cluster}}{{assert:derived.second-life.cluster == derived.eve-online.cluster}}{{assert:derived.a-tale-in-the-desert.cluster == derived.eve-online.cluster}} Ultima Online grew skills through practice instead of classes. **Planets in the hand.** [[populous|Populous]], [[simearth|SimEarth]], [[from-dust|From Dust]], and [[terra-nil|Terra Nil]] hold together on terraforming, god powers, habitat, and water, and the evolution toys [[simlife|SimLife]] and [[species-alre|Species: Artificial Life, Real Evolution]] join them.{{assert:derived.simearth.cluster == derived.populous.cluster}}{{assert:derived.from-dust.cluster == derived.populous.cluster}}{{assert:derived.terra-nil.cluster == derived.populous.cluster}}{{assert:derived.simlife.cluster == derived.populous.cluster}}{{assert:derived.species-alre.cluster == derived.populous.cluster}} SimEarth, built with James Lovelock as adviser, prices its interventions (chapter 2) at 50 units of energy per square of terrain and 500 per terraforming device, and From Dust was designed by Eric Chahi. **Programs.** SpaceChem, from chapter 2, holds together with [[opus-magnum|Opus Magnum]], [[the-farmer-was-replaced|The Farmer Was Replaced]], [[bitburner|Bitburner]], and [[autonauts|Autonauts]].{{assert:derived.opus-magnum.cluster == derived.spacechem.cluster}}{{assert:derived.the-farmer-was-replaced.cluster == derived.spacechem.cluster}}{{assert:derived.bitburner.cluster == derived.spacechem.cluster}}{{assert:derived.autonauts.cluster == derived.spacechem.cluster}} In each, the player writes or records a program and the game runs it. ## Series and descendants

Series stay together. [[simcity-1989|SimCity]] and [[simcity-4|SimCity 4]], The Sims and The Sims 4, Railroad Tycoon and [[transport-tycoon|Transport Tycoon]], and Anno 1800 and [[anno-117|Anno 117]] each share a cluster in every subsample or nearly every one.{{assert:cocluster.simcity-1989.simcity-4 >= 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.the-sims.the-sims-4 >= 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.railroad-tycoon.transport-tycoon >= 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.anno-1800.anno-117 >= 0.9}} So do [[elite|Elite]] (1984) and [[elite-dangerous|Elite Dangerous]] (2014), thirty years apart, on generated star systems, trade, and a ship flown by hand.{{assert:cocluster.elite.elite-dangerous >= 0.9}}

Frostpunk's two games hold together as well, though both are filed as city builders and their family is mostly grand strategy.{{assert:field.frostpunk.subgenre == city-builder}}{{assert:field.frostpunk-2.subgenre == city-builder}}

[[frostpunk|Frostpunk]] (2018) is a survival city builder around a single coal generator. Temperature falls on a schedule toward a great storm, the generator heats a circle whose range sets the city's layout, and the Book of Laws offers early compromises such as child labour and extended shifts that open harsher options later, tracked by meters of hope and discontent. Its closest neighbour in the catalog is [[against-the-storm|Against the Storm]], another city builder run against a clock.{{assert:derived.against-the-storm.cluster == derived.frostpunk.cluster}} Its laws and its scenario stories, told through events and choices, put it with the grand strategy games rather than with SimCity.{{assert:derived.frostpunk.cluster == derived.europa-universalis-4.cluster}}{{assert:derived.frostpunk.cluster != derived.simcity-1989.cluster}}

[[frostpunk-2|Frostpunk 2]] (2024) moves from single buildings to districts and from one leader's decree to a council. A hundred delegates, each tied to a faction, vote on every proposed law; most laws need 51 votes and laws that expand the Steward's power need two thirds. Promises made to win a vote and then broken anger factions, and tension between factions can escalate to civil war. ## Reading a family

A family is a reading list. Take Frostpunk 2 and its two closest relatives. With Europa Universalis IV it shares interest groups, policy choices, stability and unrest, and a tech tree; with Tropico 6 it shares interest groups, policy choices, and stability, plus elections, but not the tech tree.{{assert:cocluster.frostpunk-2.tropico-6 >= 0.9}} Europa Universalis IV also has parts Frostpunk 2 does not, and two of them bear directly on a council game. Opinion modifiers make each faction's attitude an itemized sum of named, decaying entries, so a player can read why a group feels the way it does. Causal tooltips, the nested breakdowns of chapter 5, explain any derived number by its named modifiers. Neither record can say whether those parts would suit Frostpunk 2's design. They are the parts its nearest neighbours use to make a political system readable, and a designer working on a game in that family would want to know about them.

The reading also runs from a game filed outside its family. Terra Nil shares a cluster with Populous and From Dust in every subsample or nearly every one.{{assert:cocluster.terra-nil.populous >= 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.terra-nil.from-dust >= 0.9}} Both make the land the thing the player works on: terraforming is core in each, and in From Dust water and material interactions are core as well, so terrain erodes, floods, and hardens without the player touching it. Terra Nil keeps water and terraforming as supporting parts and counts restoration tile by tile.

The same reading works in any family. Princess Maker 2 ends with one of 74 careers selected by eight years of accumulated attributes; Papers, Please ends with one of twenty, reached by scripted branches from bribes and rule-bending.

## How much genre explains

Compare the clusters with the subgenre labels the catalog gives each game. The adjusted Rand index between clusters and subgenres is {{stat:cluster_subgenre_ari}}, where 1 would mean the clusters reproduce the labels exactly and 0 is what chance gives. Normalized mutual information, which asks how much knowing a game's cluster tells you about its subgenre, is {{stat:cluster_subgenre_nmi}}, and chance alone scores well on it: when the subgenre labels are shuffled across games {{stat:cluster_permutations}} times, it averages {{stat:cluster_subgenre_null_nmi}}, because many small clusters share some information with any labeling. No shuffle matches the real labels (p = {{stat:p_cluster_subgenre_nmi}}). Corrected for chance, as adjusted mutual information, agreement is {{stat:cluster_subgenre_ami}} on the same scale as the Rand index. ## Where the method is weak

The families are as good as the decompositions they are built from, and those have limits that the clustering cannot see.

<details class="disclosure"><summary>four limits, with examples</summary>

Games with very few mechanics look alike by what they lack. [[game-of-life|Conway's Game of Life]] has {{derived:game-of-life.mechanic_count}} mechanics, one of them the turn structure it shares with [[hamurabi|Hamurabi]] and [[oregon-trail|The Oregon Trail]], and it sits in a cluster with them in nearly every subsample.{{assert:derived.game-of-life.mechanic_count <= 3}}{{assert:cocluster.hamurabi.game-of-life >= 0.9}} That cluster is stable and is mostly small early programs; it says little about their designs.

Belonging together is not transitive. Ultima Online and Second Life each share a cluster with EVE Online in at least nine of ten subsamples, and with each other in about four of five; Screeps belongs with Second Life and shares a cluster with EVE in about three of four.{{assert:cocluster.ultima-online.eve-online >= 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.second-life.eve-online >= 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.ultima-online.second-life < 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.ultima-online.second-life > 0.7}}{{assert:cocluster.screeps.second-life >= 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.screeps.eve-online < 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.screeps.eve-online > 0.6}}

Some clusters hold together less well than the rest. The one that holds the survival games keeps a much lower overlap with its best match across subsamples than the stable families do. Its core stays together: Minecraft and Subnautica share a cluster in every subsample. The farming games [[stardew-valley|Stardew Valley]] and [[harvest-moon|Harvest Moon]] always land together, and join Minecraft in only about half the subsamples.{{assert:cocluster.minecraft.subnautica >= 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.stardew-valley.harvest-moon >= 0.9}}{{assert:cocluster.minecraft.stardew-valley > 0.3}}{{assert:cocluster.minecraft.stardew-valley < 0.6}}

The clustering also sees only which mechanics a game uses. It ignores scale, tuning, and how the parts are wired together. Two games with the same parts and very different numbers, a gentle farm and a punishing one, look identical to it. And every decomposition was written by one model rater against the taxonomy's definitions, so a different rater could list different parts for the same game. A second, independent set of decompositions would show how much the families move when the rater changes.
