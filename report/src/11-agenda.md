Every opportunity carries a falsifiable prediction with a resolve-by date and criteria a stranger could check. The first resolves on {{stat:first_prediction_resolves_long}}. **What a resolution would show.** A prediction tests where a technique lands only if its criteria require that technique. Every prediction's criteria therefore end with a sentence that names the technique class that counts and calls the prediction one of two kinds, and the build counts them. A technique test is one whose criteria only the technique class the opportunity applies can meet: a language model that ranks authored events ([[drama-aware-storyteller]]), or a trained policy that drives a physics character ([[neural-npc-motion]]). The class need not be learned; [[qd-scenario-libraries]] requires quality-diversity search, an evolutionary method, because that is the technique it applies. A feature test is one whose criteria classical, symbolic, or operations-research methods can also meet, and its sentence names such a method. {{stat:n_predictions_technique_test}} of the {{stat:n_predictions}} predictions are technique tests and {{stat:n_predictions_feature_test}} are feature tests.{{assert:n_predictions_unclassified == 0}}{{assert:derived.drama-aware-storyteller.prediction_test == technique}}{{assert:derived.neural-npc-motion.prediction_test == technique}}{{assert:derived.qd-scenario-libraries.prediction_test == technique}} Only the technique tests score where techniques land. A confirmed feature test shows that the feature shipped, and which technique built it only where the evidence says so; several feature-test sentences name that evidence, such as release notes saying that a language model writes the answers.

The three legibility tools are feature tests for two different reasons. [[branch-preview-forecasts]] and [[bottleneck-explainer]] need no language and no learned component: forked runs of the game's own simulation under different random draws, or perturbations of it, meet their criteria, and their sentences name classical Monte Carlo runs and sensitivity analysis as counting.{{assert:derived.branch-preview-forecasts.prediction_test == feature}}{{assert:derived.bottleneck-explainer.prediction_test == feature}} [[causal-why-query]] needs free-text questions answered from the player's save state. A language model is the likelier way to build that, but a parser for a fixed grammar of questions over a cause-linked event log, answering from templates, also meets the criteria as written, so the prediction stays a feature test, and its sentence says that a confirmation shows a language model landed only if the release notes say one writes the answers.{{assert:derived.causal-why-query.prediction_test == feature}}

**Generic diffusion.** A prediction of the form "a game will ship X" can come true through general adoption of AI without the mechanism its opportunity proposes. General adoption is common and mostly shallow. A little under a fifth of the games released on Steam in 2025, counted in July, disclosed generative AI [@src-totallyhuman-2025-steam-genai-count], and among the 45 disclosing titles that a rough revenue estimate puts at a million dollars or more, about half cited in-game art and under 5 percent cited runtime generative systems [@src-totallyhuman-2025-steam-ai-revenue]. Some predictions forecast the spread of a feature that already exists, as [[generative-decoration-catalogs]] does when it excludes [[inzoi]], and most are feature tests, so a confirmation can come from methods that owe nothing to the catalogued techniques.{{assert:n_predictions_feature_test > n_predictions_technique_test}} Each prediction therefore has a baseline, set before any resolved and kept in `report/predictions.yaml`: the reference class a resolver counts, named by Steam tags and the size bar of the criteria; the generic version of the event, the same event arriving through general adoption of AI or through classical methods; and what a confirmation's evidence must show to count for the thesis.{{assert:n_prediction_baselines == n_predictions}} For [[causal-why-query]] the generic version is a chatbot that answers from a wiki or general knowledge, and only answers drawn from the player's own save count for the thesis. The criteria alone decide the outcome. The baseline decides what a confirmation shows, and a confirmation without the thesis evidence is recorded as generic diffusion. The build fails when a live prediction has no baseline, when a baseline names an opportunity that is missing or deprecated, or when a reference class names a review count that the criteria do not state.

No probabilities were added. One attached now would come from the rater who wrote the prediction, another self-generated judgment; the base rates above are counts, not forecasts. When a prediction comes due, its resolver counts the reference class again: how many of its titles shipped the feature in the window and, of those on Steam, how many disclose generative AI, which is the generic-diffusion rate on that day (`tasks/resolve-prediction.md`).

The evidence is also asymmetric, as the note under the prediction table says: a refutation can reflect cost, schedules, or reception rather than a wrong thesis, and a confirmation supports the thesis only as far as the criteria and the baseline require the opportunity's own mechanism. The dates are the rater's judgment, not derived from studio lead times. The {{stat:n_predictions_first_date}} predictions due first leave little more than a year from when they were written, so they test mainly patches, expansions, and games already in production.{{assert:n_predictions_first_date >= 5}}

Predictions that depend on how a game is built inside, such as model calls only on salient events ([[salience-gated-llm-agents]]), a learned weather emulator ([[learned-weather-layer]]), or a mean-field crowd model ([[mean-field-crowds]]), name public evidence that counts: developer documentation, postmortems, patch notes, or behavior modders have verified. Outsiders often cannot see architecture, so these predictions risk false negatives (the feature shipped, but no public source says how it works) and false positives (marketing copy that overstates it, accepted by a verifier at face value). The outcome vocabulary has no term for the first case. *Ambiguous* needs evidence that a criterion cannot be checked as written, and *refuted* needs a criterion shown unmet, so a prediction that no public evidence can decide either way would be forced into one of them. An *undetermined* outcome, distinct from refuted, needs a vocabulary change by ADR; until one exists, a verifier facing that case should record it as ambiguous and say in the note that no public evidence exists.

### 11.1 Agenda

1. **Instrument a simulation for explanation.** Build the event log with cause links that why-queries, branch previews, and chronicles all need, and measure explanation faithfulness against human audit.
2. **Measure legibility in players, not only in rubrics.** The legibility and emergence scores here are judgments by a single model rater; a shared protocol for measuring how quickly players form correct causal models would let designers compare mechanics empirically.
3. **Connect techniques to the economic core.** Resource and production mechanics are the most used family in the catalog and, per mechanic, among the least reached by recorded technique edges, and logistics mechanics fare no better (§8.2).{{assert:most_used_family == Resources & production}}{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.resources-production >= 16}}{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.logistics-networks >= 16}} Balance search, recipe generation, and bottleneck explanation are tractable first steps.
4. **Separate authoritative and generated layers.** Develop and benchmark architectures where learned models render, narrate, or propose, and symbolic state decides.
5. **Extend the dataset.** Add mobile and non-English simulation traditions, score mechanics with a second, calibrated rater (`calibration/`, `tasks/rate-scores.md`), resolve the verification queue, and record design tensions with evidence from postmortems.
6. **Rate the technique layer twice.** Every technique record, `affects` edge, maturity judgment, and opportunity score comes from one rater. A second coder should label the seeded sample of {{stat:n_second_coding_edges}} edges by effect, with the coding rule of §8.1 and the sheets `scripts/coding.py` writes, which also ask for the family and admission tests of the sampled mechanics (§2.6, `tasks/second-coding.md`), so that the build reports Cohen's kappa and Krippendorff's alpha overall and per effect; derive edges blind for a sample of mechanics and report recall against the existing ones, starting with the most used unreached mechanics; re-rate maturity, TRL, and horizon for a sample of techniques, including the three TRL mismatches of §7.1; and score at least the top fifteen opportunities. Until then the effect counts, the exposure ranking, and the order in §9 are one rater's.
7. **Sharpen the predictions.** Have a reader other than the original rater check which predictions are technique tests and which feature tests, and check the baselines and reference classes in `report/predictions.yaml`, which the same rater wrote; have independent raters attach a probability to each before it comes due; and add an undetermined outcome to the vocabulary for predictions that public evidence cannot decide.

### 11.2 How to extend this report

The report is generated. To add a game, mechanic, technique, or opportunity, follow `AGENTS.md`; tables, counts, figures, rankings, and this section's prediction list update on the next build. To change an argument, edit the prose in `report/src/`; every dataset number in it is a token, so the text cannot fall out of date with the data.
