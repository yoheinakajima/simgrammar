## 1. Introduction

A simulation game is a game whose state changes by rules the player did not author and does not fully control. The player builds a road and the traffic is the city's reply; the player sets a tax and the citizens decide whether to stay. Everything distinctive about the form follows from that gap between intent and outcome: the pleasure of watching a system respond, the frustration of not understanding why it did, and the stories players tell about runs no designer scripted.

The genre is old and large. Its lineage runs from text programs on mainframes (a grain-and-population ledger in [[hamurabi]], a wagon party's rations in [[oregon-trail]]) through [[simcity-1989]] and [[the-sims]] to [[dwarf-fortress]], [[factorio]], and [[eve-online]]. Its methods are less visible than its titles. Designers share a working vocabulary (needs meters, production chains, storytellers, overlays), and pattern collections and ontologies name many of its parts (§1.1), but none of the prior work we found records, in files a reader can query, which rule-systems shipped games combine, how each one fails, and which are near their ceilings.

That map matters now because the ceilings are moving. For forty years the depth of a simulation game was bounded by a few constraints: how much compute a consumer machine could spend per tick, how much content a studio could author, how much hidden state a player could understand, how tightly a multiplayer game had to stay deterministic, and how believable a rule-driven agent could be. Results from 2019 to 2026 in learned world models, language-model agents, learned physics, accelerated simulation, automated design, and on-device inference press on each of those constraints, unevenly and with new costs.

This report does four things:

1. **Catalogs** {{count:game}} simulation games across {{stat:n_subgenres}} subgenres, from {{stat:year_min}} to {{stat:year_max}}, and decomposes each into the mechanics it uses ({{count:edge:uses}} typed game-to-mechanic edges, each marked core or supporting, with implementation notes for core mechanics).
2. **Analyzes** {{count:mechanic}} mechanics one at a time: rule-level definition, player experience, design levers, failure modes, dynamics, mathematical basis, and six anchored scores (legibility, emergence potential, authoring cost, compute cost, tuning difficulty, AI leverage).
3. **Maps** {{count:technique}} techniques, from generative world models, neural rendering, language-model agents, procedural generation, game-theoretic search, reinforcement learning, and accelerated simulation, onto those mechanics through {{stat:n_affects_edges}} `affects` edges, each typed by how the technique changes the mechanic (replaces its substrate, deepens it, enables a new form, cuts its authoring cost, explains it, tunes it, or accelerates it).
4. **Synthesizes** {{count:opportunity}} design opportunities, each with a validation experiment a small team could run and a falsifiable prediction with a resolve-by date.

The title's "grammar" is meant in a weak sense: a shared, machine-readable vocabulary of rule-systems with typed relations among them, in which any simulation game can be described as the set of mechanics it uses. It has no production rules, no constraints that decide which combinations are well formed, and no executable semantics; nothing in it generates or runs a game, and it is closer to a taxonomy with relations than to a formal grammar. Its relations are sparse. {{count:edge:requires}} `requires`, {{count:edge:specializes}} `specializes`, {{count:edge:synergy}} `synergy`, and {{count:edge:tension}} `tension` edges join the {{count:mechanic}} mechanics, {{stat:relation_edges_per_mechanic}} per mechanic on average (median {{stat:median_relation_edges_per_mechanic}}, at most {{stat:max_relation_edges_per_mechanic}}); {{stat:n_mechanics_no_relation}} mechanics ({{stat:pct_mechanics_no_relation}} percent) have none, and {{stat:relation_pair_density_pct}} percent of possible mechanic pairs are joined by any relation.{{assert:relation_pair_density_pct < 5}}{{assert:n_mechanics_no_relation > 0}} The `synergy` and `tension` edges were added by one model run in one pass without written criteria (§2.5), so a pair with no `tension` edge has not been shown compatible; its tension is unmeasured.

Every number in this document is rendered from the dataset at build time; the dataset hash on the title page identifies the exact version. The report, the wiki, the tables, and the graph are projections of the same YAML files, so a correction to one entity updates all of them. Formulas for the derived measures (exposure, AI leverage gap, lift, white-space gap, weighted Jaccard similarity, opportunity priority) are in Appendix E.

### 1.1 Related work

Five lines of prior work bear on this atlas: definitions of a game mechanic, vocabularies that name the parts many games share, executable descriptions of games, the modelling traditions that describe simulated systems outside games, and surveys of AI in games.

**Definitions.** Frasca defines simulating as modeling a source system through a different system that keeps some of the original's behaviors for somebody, and argues that the storytelling model of games is inaccurate and limits their analysis [@src-frasca-2003-simulation-versus-narrative]. The catalog's inclusion test asks for that kind of model: a game enters only if its core loop is driven by a model of some system whose state evolves without the player scripting each change (`AGENTS.md` §3). The MDA framework splits a game into mechanics (its components at the level of data representation and algorithms), dynamics (the run-time behavior of those mechanics acting on player input and on each other), and aesthetics (the player's emotional responses) [@src-hunicke-2004-mda]. This atlas works at the MDA mechanics level, records dynamics in each mechanic's `dynamics` field, and records aesthetics only as prose in `player_experience`. Sicart defines a mechanic as a method invoked by an agent to interact with the game world [@src-sicart-2008-defining-game-mechanics]. Järvinen, summarizing his dissertation *Games without Frontiers*, counts game mechanics, the actions players take to attain goals, as one of nine categories of game element beside components, environment, ruleset, theme, information, interface, players, and contexts [@src-jarvinen-2007-applied-ludology]. Both definitions start from what an agent or player does. The atlas starts from state: a mechanic is a rule-system that owns one kind of state and one update rule and passes four admission tests (§2.1), so [[weather]] and [[spoilage]] count as mechanics although no agent invokes them. The player actions those definitions name appear in the atlas in two places. Every mechanic's `player_interface` field records how the player reaches it (direct manipulation, placement, parameter setting, orders, programming, language, and the rest). And the control and automation family (§4.2) holds the mechanics whose own state is a standing channel from intent to action, such as an order queue ([[unit-commands]]), a map of marked work ([[designation-orders]]), or a program the game executes ([[player-programming]]); they pass the atomic test because that state persists between the player's actions and is consumed by its own update rule.{{assert:field.unit-commands.family == control-automation}}{{assert:field.designation-orders.family == control-automation}}{{assert:field.player-programming.family == control-automation}} A one-off action with no state of its own is a `player_interface` value, not a mechanic.

**Vocabularies.** The Game Ontology Project builds a hierarchy of concepts from black-box analysis of many games, with interface, rules, goals, entities, and entity manipulation at the top; each entry names a parent, children, part-of elements, and strong and weak example games [@src-zagal-2005-game-ontology-project]. Game design patterns describe recurring interactions, each with its consequences, advice on using it, and its relations to superior patterns, subpatterns, and conflicting patterns [@src-bjork-2003-game-design-patterns], and Björk and Holopainen's book collects them as a shared vocabulary for designers [@src-bjork-2005-patterns-in-game-design]. Operational logics connect the abstract operations that drive a system's state with how its authors and players understand them [@src-mateas-2009-defining-operational-logics]. Machinations diagrams model a game's internal economy and can be run to simulate it before it is built [@src-adams-2012-game-mechanics-advanced-game-design]. Each of these does something the atlas does not. The ontology places every concept in a hierarchy of parents, children, and parts, where the atlas files its {{count:mechanic}} mechanics in {{stat:n_families}} families one level deep, with {{count:edge:specializes}} `specializes` edges for narrower forms and no part-of relation. The patterns relate each pattern to the patterns that implement it and those it conflicts with, and advise on its use; the atlas relates mechanics by {{count:edge:requires}} `requires`, {{count:edge:synergy}} `synergy`, and {{count:edge:tension}} `tension` edges, and gives each mechanic's levers and failure modes in place of advice. Operational logics analyze code and communication together, and the atlas reads no code. Machinations diagrams execute, and the atlas records a mechanic's feedback polarity and mathematical basis as categorical fields that cannot be run. What the atlas adds is narrower: a decomposition of catalog games into mechanics that admission tests have filtered. The ontology admits graded membership through strong and weak examples; the atlas admits a mechanic only if it passes all four tests, then tags each of {{count:game}} catalog games with the mechanics it uses ({{count:edge:uses}} typed edges, each core or supporting). Prevalence, co-occurrence, clusters, and white space (§5) are computed from those edges, in open files a reader can query.

**Executable descriptions.** VGDL describes a two-dimensional arcade game as a set of sprites, a level map, the effects of collisions between sprite types, and termination conditions, and its library plays any description it parses [@src-schaul-2013-video-game-description-language]. GVGAI built a benchmark on VGDL games, with tracks for agents that play unseen games and for generators of levels and rules [@src-perez-liebana-2018-gvgai]. Ludii describes games in ludemes, high-level game concepts that a person can read, and runs them for game-playing agents [@src-piette-2019-ludii]. The procedural content generation textbook calls each rule generator, whose encoding usually starts from one genre, an executable micro-theory of game design [@src-shaker-2016-procedural-content-generation]. These descriptions can be played, searched, and generated, and atlas records cannot. The atlas trades that for coverage and grain: it describes shipped simulation games and public research demonstrations, from [[hamurabi]] to [[eve-online]], as named rule-systems, each with its levers, failure modes, dynamics, and six anchored scores.

**Simulation modelling.** Older traditions describe simulated systems for analysis and training rather than play, and the atlas borrows their vocabulary. System dynamics models a system as levels, or stocks, that rates of flow fill and drain, joined in feedback loops; Forrester placed social systems in the class of multi-loop nonlinear feedback systems [@src-forrester-1971-counterintuitive-behavior-social-systems], and Sterman's textbook teaches the method through causal loop diagrams, stocks and flows, and positive feedback [@src-sterman-2000-business-dynamics]. The six layers of §4.1 take their terms from it: aggregate stocks as a substrate, reinforcing and balancing loops as dynamics, and the dominant loop polarity that each mechanic's `feedback` field records. Agent-based modelling grows collective behavior from individual agents following local rules. Epstein and Axtell's Sugarscape adds rules for seasons, reproduction, combat, trade, and pollution to one landscape of agents and watches migration, markets, and histories emerge [@src-epstein-1996-growing-artificial-societies], and the ODD protocol standardizes how such models are described, in seven elements that include the entities with their state variables and the schedule on which processes update them; its authors propose it as a common language for describing any simulation model [@src-grimm-2020-odd-protocol-second-update]. Gaming simulation, the tradition of policy and training games that Duke's *Gaming: The Future's Language* helped found, treats a simulation game as a model of a reference system built from roles, rules, and resources [@src-duke-2014-back-to-the-future-of-gaming]. These traditions describe one model at a time, completely enough to run or replicate it: an ODD document names every state variable and update, and a stock-and-flow model every equation. The atlas describes many games partially and at a coarser grain. A mechanic, which owns one kind of state and one update rule, corresponds roughly to one ODD submodel or one stock-and-flow structure, named so that it can be recognized across games; a game's decomposition says which of these it contains, not how they are parameterized or scheduled. Sugarscape's rules, introduced in turn into one model, are close to what the atlas calls mechanics. The atlas applies these traditions' terms to entertainment games and does not test them; it contributes an index across games, not a modelling method.

**AI in games.** Yannakakis and Togelius organize the field by what AI does in games: playing them, generating their content, and modeling their players [@src-yannakakis-2025-artificial-intelligence-and-games]. The procedural content generation textbook organizes generators by method and by the content they produce [@src-shaker-2016-procedural-content-generation]. Both explain how each method works, which the atlas leaves to them. The atlas indexes the same kind of results by the mechanic each one changes. Every technique carries `affects` edges ({{stat:n_affects_edges}} in all), each typed by whether the technique replaces a mechanic's substrate, deepens it, enables a new form, cuts its authoring cost, explains it, tunes it, or accelerates it, with a maturity judged for games, so the build can count the mechanics no technique reaches and the uses they carry (§8). Each opportunity then ends in a prediction with a resolve-by date ({{stat:n_predictions}} in all, the first due {{stat:first_prediction_resolves_long}}). {{stat:n_predictions_technique_test}} of them are technique tests, whose criteria only the opportunity's own technique class can meet, and only these score the atlas's reading of where techniques will land against what ships; the other {{stat:n_predictions_feature_test}} are feature tests, whose criteria classical or symbolic methods can also meet, so they score whether a feature ships, not which technique built it (§11).{{assert:n_predictions_unclassified == 0}}

**Method.** The atlas also adds a way of keeping such a vocabulary consistent as it grows. Every record names the run that created it and the runs that reviewed it, and every run names its operator, its model when an agent ran it, and its method (§2.5). Numbers reach this paper's prose only through tokens the build fills in from the data, and {{stat:n_report_sentences_asserted}} of its sentences carry assertions that fail the build when the data stop supporting the ranking, sign, threshold, or grouping they state. The build checks that the prose agrees with the records; it does not check whether a record is right about a game or whether a reading of the data is valid (§2.5, Appendix C).
