## 8. Where the techniques land

### 8.1 How techniques change mechanics

Each `affects` edge records one of seven effects. Across {{stat:n_affects_edges}} edges the most common effect is *deepens* ({{stat:n_effect_deepens}} edges): the technique makes an existing mechanic richer, larger, or more varied without changing its role.{{assert:top_effect == Deepens}} Within this atlas, explanation is the rarest coded effect ({{stat:n_effect_explains}} edges).{{assert:rarest_effect == Explains}}

{{table:effect-distribution}}

**Coding rule.** An edge carries one effect, the primary one: the change its note describes to what the mechanic can be. When more than one fits, the first that applies in this order is used. *Replaces substrate* when the technique swaps how the mechanic computes its state while its role in play stays. *Enables new form* when it makes possible a version the old implementation could not produce at any cost. *Explains* when its output is addressed to the player's understanding of the mechanic's state or causes. *Deepens* when the same mechanic gains fidelity, scale, or variety at runtime. *Accelerates* when the same output costs less runtime compute. For work done at design time, *cuts authoring cost* when it makes the content the mechanic consumes, and *tunes* when it sets or tests the mechanic's parameters. The order was written down after the edges were coded, by the same rater, so it states the convention the edges were meant to follow; whether they follow it is untested until a second rater relabels a sample (§11.1). Collapsing the three effects that overlap most (deepens, enables new form, and cuts authoring cost) into one leaves explanation the rarest,{{assert:rarest_effect_collapsed == Explains}} and the family order by exposure (§8.2) correlates with the uncollapsed one at ρ = {{stat:rho_family_exposure_collapsed_effects}}.{{assert:rho_family_exposure_collapsed_effects >= 0.9}}

The {{stat:n_effect_explains}} explanation edges come from {{stat:n_explains_techniques}} techniques, {{stat:n_effect_explains_top_technique}} of them from one prototype, [[llm-explanation-of-state]].{{assert:explains_top_technique == llm-explanation-of-state}}{{assert:field.llm-explanation-of-state.maturity == prototype}}

{{table:explains-edges}}

A count this small and this concentrated describes the atlas more than the field. Two features of the method push it down: a technique enters only if a result changes a named game mechanic (§7.1), so general explainability research (attribution, causal tracing, question answering over logs) is absent unless someone has applied it to a game; and an edge carries one label, so a technique that explains as it deepens may be coded as deepening. Scarcity in the record may also reflect low demand for explanation, or its difficulty. What the count does show is that the recorded edges concentrate on generating content and replacing substrates (rule-based behavior with language models, numerical physics with learned dynamics), and that few recorded results address explaining a simulation to the person playing it, although the components that would need (retrieval over structured logs, constrained generation, summarization) exist.

### 8.2 Exposure by family

Technique exposure is a deployment-weighted heuristic. For each mechanic it sums, over the techniques with an `affects` edge to it, the technique's maturity weight times the edge's effect weight; a family's exposure is the sum over its mechanics, and its exposure per mechanic divides that by the number of mechanics. The weights are read from `vocab/rubrics.yaml`:

{{table:exposure-weights}}

Maturity weights run from {{stat:w_exposure_maturity_min}} for research to {{stat:w_exposure_maturity_max}} for a genre norm, so the index measures where deployable results point and down-weights early-stage work by construction. Effect weights run from {{stat:w_exposure_effect_min}} to {{stat:w_exposure_effect_max}}, counting a change to what a mechanic can be above tuning it or speeding it up. Both are judgments, not estimates; the checks after the tables test how far the readings depend on them.

A rendering technique can affect a rule-system mechanic only through what the mechanic shows or the content it draws on. [[neural-rendering-shaders]] has edges into hands-on skill control (more real frames on the same hardware), the day-night cycle (lighting that follows player construction), and decoration (more decor resident in memory), and each edge's note says the mechanic's state and update rule are unchanged.{{assert:field.neural-rendering-shaders.maturity == shipped-mainstream}} Neural rendering and [[real-time-video-restyling]], whose records say they leave simulation state alone, are treated as presentation-only in the checks below.

An edge exists only where a published result names the mechanic, and its absence is weak evidence (§2.6). The edges were written by one rater and have not been checked for recall against an independent reading, so these figures more likely overstate than understate how much of the catalog no published result reaches. Many unreached mechanics also have a technique proposed for them by an opportunity (§9). The same rater wrote the proposals.

In this atlas, then, recorded technique edges concentrate on what a simulation game shows and says, not on the rule-systems most simulation games are built from. How far that describes AI research rather than the atlas's selection of it is open, since the set was gathered by searching for results that name game mechanics (§7.1). If the pattern holds for the field, it is partly sensible (production chains and budgets are cheap and legible already) and partly a gap: the economic core is where balance work, legibility problems, and authoring of recipes and scenarios consume design time.

### 8.4 Judgment versus evidence

Each mechanic carries a judged `ai_leverage` score. It correlates with technique exposure at ρ = {{stat:rho_ai_leverage_exposure}}.{{assert:rho_ai_leverage_exposure >= 0.3}} This is a consistency check, not independent agreement: the original model rater scored leverage, wrote the technique `affects` edges, and judged technique maturity (§2.6). Independent second ratings cover part of the mechanic scores; the technique links and maturity judgments retain their original authorship. The leverage-gap table lists where they disagree: mechanics judged highly open to AI that no technique in the set yet addresses.

{{table:leverage-gap}}

The gap is a difference of two coarse quantities, a five-level score and a weighted edge count, so its order is a list of candidates, not a ranking. {{stat:n_leverage_gap_tied_top}} mechanics tie exactly for the largest gap, and {{stat:n_leverage_gap_tied_top_shown}} share it at the one decimal the inputs support; within a tie, rows are ordered by how many games use the mechanic. The rank interval column shows how far each row moves when every leverage score and technique maturity shifts by up to one level and every weight by up to {{stat:perturbation_weight_spread_pct}} percent.{{assert:n_leverage_gap_tied_top > 1}} Modding interfaces, blueprints, romance and family systems, creature care, diagnosis, product design, history generation, and rule-checking inspection are each judged ready for substantial change, and none has a recorded technique edge in this dataset.{{assert:derived.modding-api.technique_exposure == 0}}{{assert:derived.blueprints.technique_exposure == 0}}{{assert:derived.history-generation.technique_exposure == 0}}{{assert:derived.rule-checking-inspection.technique_exposure == 0}} Some are covered implicitly by general techniques (code generation for modding, language models for romance dialogue), but none has a cited, game-specific result.

### 8.5 Authoritative state versus presentation

The techniques divide by whether they touch authoritative simulation state. Those that change only presentation have shipped (neural upscaling and frame generation, asset generation, motion synthesis) or reached prototypes (restyling); they preserve determinism or can be made reproducible by storing what they generate.{{assert:field.real-time-video-restyling.maturity == prototype}} Removing the two whose records say they never touch state barely moves the family order in total (ρ = {{stat:rho_family_exposure_no_presentation}}).{{assert:rho_family_exposure_no_presentation >= 0.95}} Those that would replace physics, fields, or the world with learned prediction remain prototypes or research in games. The shipped exceptions that reach state are narrow and controlled: cached model outputs as rules ([[llm-rule-adjudication]]), language-model characters that choose actions in [[inzoi]] ([[generative-agents]], [[on-device-language-models]]), and opponents ([[deep-rl-npc-policies]], [[monte-carlo-tree-search]]). Of these, only the language characters break reproducibility outright.{{assert:field.generative-agents.determinism_impact == breaks}}{{assert:field.deep-rl-npc-policies.determinism_impact == mitigable}}{{assert:field.monte-carlo-tree-search.determinism_impact == mitigable}}{{assert:field.llm-rule-adjudication.determinism_impact == mitigable}} The pattern suggests a design principle that recurs through §9: keep the simulation symbolic, typed, and logged, and let learned models read it, explain it, decorate it, and propose changes to it through validated interfaces.
